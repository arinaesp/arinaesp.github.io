Bridging Code Mechanics and Language Acquisition

When you look at modern EdTech for young learners, you see language apps on one side and coding games on the other—with zero overlap in between. axo was born from a simple, untested question: what happens when code is the medium through which language is learned?

I keep coming back to this thought... how a five-year-old picks up a new word in one game, one try, while an adult in my general and business English classes just needs a little more time with the same word before it settles in. I've taught both. For years. And it was never about intelligence — it's just how each of them is wired to learn at that age.

I've been carrying this idea for about a year and a half now — turning it over, adding a piece here, letting it sit untouched for weeks, then coming back to it with something new. Somewhere alongside all the teaching, there was this other, quieter dream running underneath: I wanted to become "techy." Not for a title. I wanted to understand how machines actually think, how they work underneath — that mechanism itself has always felt fascinating to me, almost magnetic, like something I wanted to sit down and talk to, and build things together with. I didn't know what shape that would take. I just knew I wanted something that could take the routine parts off my hands, or let me get an idea out of my head and into something real before it lost its shape. I think what I was actually dreaming about, without having a name for it yet, was something closer to task imagination — you imagine the thing, and it starts to exist.

And further back than that — art school, from age 11 to 15, an experimental pilot program I had to test my way into — I think something in that training was quietly getting me ready for this too, even if I couldn't have told you that at the time. I spent those years learning to draw across every technique they could throw at us, alongside art history, and for a long time I assumed that made me a "frontend person" — visual first, design first. Turns out that's not quite it. My actual core is closer to the opposite: I'm an introvert, and the place I feel most like myself is a terminal, writing backend code. But it's really the same instinct either way — I paint with a brush, or I paint with code and watch the result show up on localhost first, before anyone else sees it. Same act, different material.

This article is my attempt to lay all of that out honestly — the dream, the research behind it, the pedagogy it's actually grounded in, and how the thing got built, solo, research through code.

1. The Problem I Kept Running Into

Two things kept surfacing while I dug into this, and together they're why I think the timing is genuinely right for something like axo.

English proficiency here is actually improving, just not where the official numbers show it yet. From what I see in classrooms, kids in early grades — at least in the bigger cities — are already speaking English with each other, which wasn't the case when I started teaching. A lot of that seems to come from social media: trendy videos are often in English now, and that kind of daily, unstructured exposure moves faster than anything a textbook can do. One of the biggest drivers I actually see with my own students is anime — a huge number of them watch it in English, sometimes with subtitles, sometimes without, and it shows in how naturally certain phrases come out of them. The official rankings haven't caught up to that yet — the EF English Proficiency Index (EF EPI) 2025, built from 2.2 million test-takers worldwide, still puts Kyrgyzstan at 101st out of 123 countries, in the "very low" band, alongside the rest of the region: Kazakhstan (107th), Uzbekistan (104th), Tajikistan (110th). But it's worth holding both things at once — the index measures adults who self-selected into an online test, and it's simply too slow to reflect what's shifting in classrooms and on phone screens right now.

Telegram is where everyone already is. Telegram passed 1 billion monthly active users in March 2025, and 450–500 million of them open it every single day — roughly half the monthly base coming back daily, which is an unusually strong number for a messaging app. A Levada Center survey from March 2024 found around 51% of Russian citizens use Telegram regularly, and Russia is one of Telegram's biggest markets by raw user count. People open the app 20+ times a day, on average, and spend about 40 minutes a day inside it. So the way I see it: this is a platform full of people who are already there, every day, without me having to convince anyone to download something new. That's the whole reason axo is a Telegram Mini App instead of a native iOS/Android app.

Mini Apps make this easier, not just possible. Telegram's Mini App ecosystem has grown a lot alongside the platform itself, and the appeal is zero-install friction — a Mini App opens instantly inside a chat someone's already in, using the Telegram login they already have. No signup form, no app store, no permissions popup. For a kids' product, that matters more than it sounds like it should, because the real barrier was never whether the kid is interested. It's whether the parent is willing to install yet another app on their phone.

And nobody else is actually doing what axo does. I went through the leading children's coding platforms — Scratch/ScratchJr, CodeMonkey, Tynker, Kodable, CodeCombat, Hopscotch, Grasshopper — and a pattern showed up fast: they're all built for kids who can already read English comfortably. Some use English-like syntax because it's convenient, not because teaching English is the point. Kodable comes closest, pairing coding with general reading-skill development, but it's not built around English as a foreign language, and vocabulary acquisition isn't part of the core loop anywhere in it. CodeCombat was actually a real inspiration for me on the coding side of things — I liked how it turned syntax practice into something that felt like a game world rather than a worksheet, and that stuck with me while I was shaping axo's own loop. But none of these platforms, CodeCombat included, use a structured language-acquisition method as the actual organizing principle of the gameplay.

Kids can learn so fast, when they're given the right kind of input, that I think we owe them products actually built to match that speed — not scaled-down adult apps with a cartoon skin on top. That's the belief underneath everything else in this article.

2. Why I Built It Around TPR

This part of the methodology isn't new to me — I learned about Total Physical Response during my TEFL certification, years before axo existed as an idea. But it's worth walking through here for anyone reading this who isn't coming from a language-teaching background, because it's the actual backbone of why axo's core loop works the way it does, not just a label I attached afterward.

Total Physical Response (TPR) is a language-teaching method from the 1960s, developed by James J. Asher, a psychology professor at San Jose State College. His research — published in The Modern Language Journal and elsewhere — tested whether pairing a spoken instruction with a physical action helps people retain new vocabulary better than instruction alone. He ran his studies with Japanese and Russian as target languages and found consistent gains in listening comprehension when learners physically acted out commands, compared to learners taught the conventional listen-and-repeat way.

One study in particular caught me — Asher and Price, in Child Development, 1967, looked specifically at age differences in how well TPR works. That mattered to me directly, because I'm not building for adults. TPR has since become a standard approach in early-childhood and beginner-level language instruction, precisely because it doesn't require the learner to already read fluently — the physical action itself carries the meaning, so comprehension can show up before production does.

I adapted this into a digital, code-based version. Instead of a teacher saying "jump" and a student physically jumping, axo shows an English command, the child types the matching code, and the on-screen character does the action right away. The action is still doing the reinforcing — it's just moved from the child's own body to the character's response on screen. Same mechanism TPR relies on (instruction → action → reinforcement), just rebuilt for a solo, asynchronous, app-based moment.

2.1 The Core Loop

axo's gameplay runs on a three-step cycle, repeated across every lesson:

1. See — a short English instruction appears (a word, a verb, a simple sentence).
2. Type — the child writes the matching code command, connecting the English word directly to its syntax.
3. Act — axo performs the action immediately, and the loop closes.

I kept this loop deliberately short. Each cycle takes seconds, which allows for a lot of repetition — repetition is a big part of what makes vocabulary stick — without it ever feeling repetitive to a kid, because watching the character actually do something is its own small reward, every time.

3. What the Product Actually Looks Like

Who it's for. I built axo around a zero-starting-point assumption: the child knows no English at all going in. That's not a limitation — it's a choice. Kids across Central Asia and the CIS region regularly reach age 10 or older without any meaningful English exposure, so designing around an assumed baseline of vocabulary or literacy would have quietly excluded exactly the kids I'm trying to reach. I've kept the target age range flexible rather than locked to a narrow band, because here, English exposure has more to do with environment than age.

Why an axolotl. Past the regeneration metaphor — an animal that can regrow a limb felt like a good stand-in for "try again, that's fine" — there's a more personal layer too. The axolotl is a beloved Minecraft character, and Minecraft's whole world is grid-based. axo's game world is also grid-based, so the mascot's design deliberately echoes that blocky Minecraft look. The character and the mechanic it lives inside weren't two separate decisions — they came from the same place, at the same time.

How lessons are structured. Lessons run in a linear sequence, and command difficulty increases progressively at each level. If a kid already knows a given command, there's a skip button — so the linear path is a sensible default, not a rule, for a child who shows up already knowing part of it.

Why the leaderboard only shows five attempts. This came directly from thinking hard about who's actually using this. A kid who can't yet easily read or parse a data table would find a long results list overwhelming, and that overwhelm pulls attention away from the game itself. So the leaderboard shows only the last five attempts per lesson — enough to notice recent progress, not enough to turn into its own reading task. Just as important: it's scoped entirely to the individual player. No global ranking, no competing against other kids. What I actually want to encourage is a child getting a little better than their own last attempt, level after level — not a race against anyone else.

4. How This Actually Got Built

Where it started. axo grew out of what I'd call my last real attempt at a goal I'd paused before: learning to program properly. I'd started coding courses once already and put them down to focus on teaching. This time I finished a hands-on program built around daily practice — build something small every day, starting with a plain calculator and a dice-roll app.

During that same stretch, I started folding small coding commands into my own English lessons with kids. The results were immediate, and honestly a little startling: a child who didn't know the English alphabet yet could not just repeat a verb, but type it on their own — and later I'd hear that same kid shouting the word as a command with friends during street races. Watching a coding command turn into a word a kid used spontaneously, completely outside the lesson, is the moment that made me actually want to build something around this, at scale, instead of just noticing it and moving on.

Finding out I'm a backend person. Working through the programming curriculum, I noticed my instincts leaned toward backend work — the command line, specifically, became a real draw, to the point where I'll admit Node.js is a little addictive. Claude and Claude Code made it possible to actually ship the Mini App on my own timeline instead of letting it sit as an idea much longer. I would have built it either way — that part was never really in question — but without them, it would have taken me a lot longer to get here.

From idea to the first ten lessons. Once the technical foundation was in place, the first real build step was structuring the first ten lessons around one rule: every command needs to produce a visible, immediate result. go moves the character toward gems, collect picks them up, and a treasure counter ticks upward right in front of the kid. Each small visible outcome reinforces the word being learned. That wasn't arbitrary — verbs form the core of a beginner's early vocabulary, so a mechanic built entirely around verb-triggered actions should, in theory, reinforce exactly the vocabulary that matters most at this stage.

Why plain HTML/CSS/JS, no build step. axo is my second solo project, and keeping the stack simple — plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, no build tooling — was intentional, not a shortcut. I wanted to start simple, see what actually needed to change, get added, or get cut once real development was underway, and iterate from there, instead of locking in a more complex architecture before I actually knew what the product needed. I still think of axo as evolving and unfinished, not a one-shot release.

The hardest part wasn't the code. axo isn't, technically, a complicated product — the harder problems turned out to be design and asset-level details, not engineering ones. My favorite example: getting a character accessory — a pair of glasses — to actually sit on the character's eyes, instead of floating above the head or clipping straight through a hat, took way more iteration than any single piece of game logic did.

Why "axo." The name went through a few versions before I landed here. The earlier working name, "Axolot," had a personal layer too — it echoed my own initials, Arina Bolot becoming "Abolot," with an x swapped in for the b. I ended up preferring something shorter and cleaner, and axo is what stuck. (I only just realized, writing this, that I'm a Pisces — a water sign — and I can't help but wonder now if that had some quiet pull on why an axolotl, of all animals, felt like the right one to build around.)

What v0.56 actually means. It's not a maturity marker in the usual semantic-versioning sense — it's literally 56 logged test iterations while I built out each lesson, starting from the home page onward. A record of how much hands-on testing went into this, not a formal release milestone.

Looking back. If you asked whether I'd do it differently starting over — no. Not because nothing could be improved, but because right now the priority is building further on what already exists, not second-guessing the foundation underneath it.

5. Where I Think This Could Be Wrong

axo's central bet is that vocabulary retention improves when a language instruction is paired with a visible, immediate, self-caused action — and that this holds even when the "action" happens on screen instead of through the learner's own body, which is the traditional TPR setup. That's a reasonable extrapolation from the existing research, but I haven't validated it with my own users yet. Nobody's run a controlled comparison of vocabulary retention in axo against a standard flashcard-based EFL app or a coding-only app. That's the honest next step, once there's a large enough group of kids using it to make the comparison meaningful.

I also want to be upfront about the EF EPI numbers: they measure adult test-takers who chose to take an online English test, which makes it a proxy for national English proficiency trends, not a direct measurement of what children actually know. That's a disclosed limitation of the EF EPI methodology itself, and I'd rather say that plainly than treat 101st place as literal proof of a gap in children's education specifically. It still supports the broader point, though — that English proficiency is a widely recognized challenge across this region.

6. Where This Leaves Me

As far as I can tell from all of this, axo sits in a niche nobody else is addressing in a structured way: teaching English to kids through the same command-and-action loop that teaches them to code, built on a decades-old, actually-studied language-acquisition method instead of a gamification layer bolted onto a generic curriculum.

It's also, on purpose, a solo and iterative build — the same way most things I'm proud of tend to be. I designed the core mechanic from something I watched happen in my own classroom: a kid who couldn't read English yet typed a command on their own, and later used that same word, completely unprompted, as something to yell to friends mid-game. That moment, more than any statistic in this article, is the real reason axo exists. The research explains why the timing and the platform choice make sense. That classroom moment is why I built it at all — and it's what I keep coming back to whenever the "task imagination" I dreamed about for a year and a half finally starts looking like a real, working thing.
