axo — mission research

English through coding

Market gap, regional context, and positioning — prepared by Arina Bolotbekova

About the founder

axo is conceived, written, and built by Arina Bolotbekova, who combines two areas of direct, hands-on experience: English-language education and full-stack software development.

Arina is an IDP-certified IELTS Writing and Speaking instructor, holding additional TEFL/TESOL certification, along with teaching certificates from the language education company she has worked with. Her teaching experience spans adults relocating abroad, professionals moving into IT careers, school-age children, and teenagers preparing for standardized tests. She has written full course curricula from scratch, designing every lesson herself.

On the technical side, she is a certified full-stack developer who completed Anthropic's Claude Code developer coursework, applying that training to build axo end-to-end — architecture, branding, gameplay logic, and deployment.

The origin story

When Arina began teaching coding to children, alongside her existing English teaching, she noticed something specific: combining the two skills reinforced each other far more than teaching them separately. A child who sees a command, types it, and acts it out — the same see-type-act loop that underlies both learning to code and learning a new language — builds a habit that doesn't stay confined to a lesson. Children carried that pattern outside the classroom, using it while playing with other kids.

This observation lines up with an established methodology in language teaching itself: Total Physical Response (TPR), developed by psychologist Dr. James Asher in the 1960s. TPR pairs a spoken instruction with a physical action the learner performs, mirroring how infants absorb their first language — by watching and acting on speech long before they can produce it themselves. This same pairing is what axo's core mechanic applies to code: a child reads a command, types it, and watches axo carry it out — reinforcing English vocabulary and coding syntax through the identical loop TPR uses for spoken language.

This dual background — a certified English-language educator who is also a certified developer, with experience teaching both subjects to children — is the direct source of axo's core thesis: that language acquisition and coding literacy can be taught through the same mechanic, because they draw on the same underlying skill of learning precise, structured, rule-based communication.

1. The idea in one line

axo teaches children to code, and teaches them to read, write, and speak English through the coding commands themselves — not as two separate subjects bolted together, but as one literacy mechanic. As far as this research could establish, no existing product in Central Asia, the wider CIS region, or globally combines these two things into a single learning loop.

2. The regional gap: Central Asia and CIS

English proficiency in the region is low, and getting worse relative to the rest of the world. In the EF English Proficiency Index 2025 — scoring 123 countries and territories based on 2.2 million test-takers — the Central Asian republics cluster near the bottom of the global table:

All four sit in the "low" or "very low" proficiency bands — a large population of school-age children whose families are actively looking for ways to close an English gap, in a region where formal schooling alone isn't closing it.

Coding education in the region is informal, not productized — the clearest examples found are volunteer-run afterschool clubs rather than a scalable, localized app. One recent regional product validates half of the model: Aleem, an AI-powered English-learning app from Almaty, overtook Duolingo in the App Store within 24 hours of launch and reached #1 across five Central Asian countries within three days and 95,000 users. Its own MVP was a Telegram Mini App that gained 40,000 users and $10,000 in pre-sales in its first month — but it teaches English only, with no coding component.

No product was found in this region that does what Aleem does for English and what a coding platform does for programming, in one app, in one learning loop.

3. The global gap

Widening the search worldwide, the same pattern holds: coding education and English education exist as parallel, separate product categories, not a merged one. Coding-only platforms (Tynker, CodeMonkey, Kodable, Scratch Jr, Code.org) have no language-acquisition layer. English-only platforms (Duolingo Kids, Lingokids, ABCmouse, Studycat) have no coding layer.

The closest hybrid found is Codeyoung, an India-based live tutoring service offering coding, math, English, and science as separate subjects taught by separate mentors — subject bundling, not subject fusion, and not localized for Russian-speaking markets.

Conclusion: axo's core mechanic — English through coding, as a single fused learning loop — was not found replicated anywhere in the products surveyed, regionally or globally.

Telegram isn't the platform with the most total users worldwide — but it's where the target audience is most deeply and habitually engaged. Telegram Mini Apps are an active, proven format for exactly this kind of product: over 300 million people already play games inside Telegram Mini Apps, and Aleem's own launch trajectory validated fast regional adoption in this exact channel.

5. The literacy argument

axo's founding thesis is that coding is becoming a third foundational literacy, alongside reading and writing — not a specialized profession, but a basic skill everyone will simply know how to do.

Software is headed toward being fully democratized — a skill as ordinary as knowing how to send a text message. — Boris Cherny, creator of Claude Code, in conversation with Lauren Reeder, Sequoia AI Ascent 2026

Cherny's argument draws on the 15th-century printing press: before Gutenberg, only around 10% of Europeans could read or write, and literacy was a specialized skill performed on behalf of kings and lords who often couldn't read themselves. Within 50 years of the printing press, more literature was published in Europe than in the previous thousand years combined. Over the following centuries, global literacy climbed to around 70% — a baseline expectation for everyone, not a credentialed profession. Cherny expects coding to follow the same curve, only far faster.

If coding is genuinely on track to become a basic literacy rather than a specialist career, the earlier a child builds comfort with it, the more natural that literacy becomes — the same way early reading habits shape lifelong reading comfort. Arina shares this conviction directly: she believes coding is becoming a default literacy, and axo is her direct contribution to that future — giving children in Central Asia and the wider Russian-speaking world an early, playful head start on both languages they will need: English, and code.

6. Summary positioning statement

Central Asian and CIS children face two gaps at once: measurably low English proficiency across the region, and a coding-education landscape that is either informal or entirely unlocalized. Existing products address one gap or the other — never both together — axo's mechanic has no found precedent in this region or globally. Telegram is the right first channel: regional engagement data and Aleem's own trajectory both show a well-made Telegram Mini App can achieve fast, organic adoption in exactly this market.
